About

I am a PhD student in civil engineering at Arizona State University and a Graduate Research Associate in the School of Sustainable Engineering and the Built Environment. My doctoral research is on construction worker safety: underground pipeline incidents, heat stress, mental health, and the workforce development questions that sit underneath all three.

I came to research through the field rather than around it. Before graduate school I worked as a project engineer in Bangladesh on geotechnical testing and ground improvement, where I helped introduce prefabricated vertical drain manufacturing to the country for the first time and coordinated material approvals with BWDB, RHD, LGED, and PWD. That experience is why I care less about what a model can predict in a paper and more about whether a crew or an estimator can act on it.

It is also what drew me to the estimating problem during my master's at the University of Wyoming. On the projects I had seen, the gap between what a job was estimated to cost and what it was awarded for was treated as ordinary. I gathered bid tabulation data covering more than 1,500 highway projects across Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, and Montana and found average deviations of 19% to 48% depending on the state, concentrated in a small set of bid items rather than spread evenly. Out of that came a multi-tier machine learning framework that DOTs and contractors can follow at the item level rather than a single black-box model.

A machine learning framework for item-level cost estimation

2024 – 2026
Problem
State DOTs routinely see large gaps between the engineer's estimate and the awarded bid. WYDOT had observed deviations up to 25%, but nobody had established whether the gap was random noise or concentrated in identifiable items — and uniform estimating models treat every bid item as if it behaved the same way.
Approach
Acquired historical bid tabulation data (2017–2024) from four state DOT research offices, covering more than 1,500 projects. Screened outliers with modified Z-scores, tested group differences with ANOVA, sorted projects into high, moderate, and slight deviation clusters using K-means, then ran Pareto analysis to isolate the cost-significant items driving the deviation. Built a frequency-aware, multi-tier machine learning framework so that frequently bid items and rare ones are modeled differently.
Tools
Python with scikit-learn for clustering and gradient boosting, R, IBM SPSS.
Outcome
Average deviations of 21.41% (WYDOT), 19.10% (MDT), 48.20% (CDOT) and 24.00% (UDOT). The multi-tier framework outperformed uniform models at the item level and is designed as a procedure estimating teams can follow, not a black box. Funded by a competitive UTC research grant through CTIPS with USDOT and WYDOT as partners; published in the International Journal of Construction Management.

Tracking material deliveries from site video

2021 – 2022
Problem
Material deliveries are logged by hand at the gate, so site inventory drifts away from the paperwork and discrepancies only surface at reconciliation.
Approach
Built an automated system that detects and tracks material-carrying vehicles entering a site, updates inventory in real time, and estimates delivered quantities from the detections.
Tools
YOLOv5 object detection, a deep learning pipeline in Python, custom annotated site footage.
Outcome
83% accuracy on vehicle detection and 88% on quantity calculation. Accuracy fell in poor light and with partially occluded loads, which is the honest limit on deploying this at a real gate. Runner-up in the Builtech Fest case study competition at KUET.

A framework for how construction teams and AI divide the work

2026
Problem
Construction management literature discusses AI adoption at length but offers little structure for deciding which decisions stay with people, which move to models, and which need both.
Approach
Conducted a comprehensive review of human–AI collaboration in construction management and synthesized the findings into a paradigm framework mapping task types to collaboration modes.
Tools
AI-assisted literature screening and synthesis, with manual verification of every included source.
Outcome
Accepted to the International Conference on Industrialized Construction (IConIC 2026). The framework is conceptual and still needs testing against live project decisions.

This website

Sep 2026
Problem
My research lives in PDFs and conference proceedings. I needed one place that shows the work to recruiters, collaborators, and DOT partners, and I had never built a website.
Approach
Wrote the content first, then used an AI assistant to generate a static site, edited the markup by hand, version-controlled it on GitHub, and deployed on Vercel with automatic redeploys on every commit.
Tools
Claude for generation and revision, VS Code for editing, GitHub for version control, Vercel for hosting, TinyPNG for image compression, Chrome Lighthouse for performance and accessibility auditing.
Outcome
Built for CON 598, AI in Construction, at ASU. The build report covers hours spent, what broke, and where the AI output needed correcting.
